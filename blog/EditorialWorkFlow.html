§ The Problem

The Casualty Actuarial Society published announcements, professional content, regulatory updates, and research across Drupal and WordPress websites, but the organization was not using either platform as a traditional CMS.

The IT team managed all content posting through a HelpDesk ticket queue. Staff across editorial, marketing, and research departments authored their content in Microsoft Word, attached the .docx to a ticket, and waited for IT to post it. IT reviewed every submission before publishing. There were no editorial accounts in the CMS. There was no approval pipeline. There was no draft state. The CMS was being used as a file-attachment endpoint with a human in the middle.

The quarterly Actuarial Review was a strong example. The editorial team produced a master Word document. The same .docx used to generate the print PDF, was handed to IT who then published the entire issue, aricle by article, into WordPress. The workflow was designed around Word, not around the CMS.

§ How I Diagnosed It

My role at the CAS was centered on managing the HelpDesk and I quickly noticed the pattern. Every piece of content arrived as a Word document attached to a ticket. IT staff spent their days copying, pasting, formatting, and tagging. The CMS was installed and running, but no one outside IT had an account in it.

Staff had grown accustomed used to routing everything through IT. They saw the CMS (WordPress, Drupal) as IT's domain. IT was essentially the gatekeepers and as IT priorities shifted toward a new CRM, The system produced a predictable bottleneck. Every piece of content waited in a queue to copy, paste, format, tag, and publish.

The quarterly Actuarial Review was a costly bottleneck with strict deadlines. IT was spending entire workdays on a task the editorial team could perform themselves. They had authored the content. They knew the structure. The only missing piece was familiarity with the WordPress admin.

§ What I Built

Drupal editorial accounts and approval pipelines (shipped).

I read the Drupal documentation. The platform supports editorial workflows natively: role-based accounts, content moderation states, and approval pipelines. The solution was not a custom build. It was configuration the platform already supported.

WordPress user group coaching (shipped).

The quarterly Actuarial Review team managed content on a separate WordPress site.

I coached the editorial team on the WordPress admin. I presented on how to write posts, apply taxonomy tags, and verify that tagged articles populated correctly on the home page. The team already knew the content. They already knew the structure. They just needed to know the tool and they were relieved to have full control of the publishing pipeline.

The result: IT's workload for web postings dropped by 60%. The editorial team now publishes content directly. IT is only brought in to verify tags if something does not populate correctly on a specific page. A spot-check, not a full-cycle dependency.

Word-to-CMS plugin (designed, not deployed).

The cultural inertia behind Word was too strong for a full transition, so I designed a PHP plugin that would ingest a .docx file, map Word styles to CMS content blocks, and create a draft post ready for editorial review. The plugin would preserve the Word-first workflow staff were comfortable with while removing the copy-paste step. The design was complete. Deployment was blocked by organizational priorities. Our business officer preferred to maintain existing processes.

§ How I Verified the Results

The Drupal editorial accounts went live, implemented by the vendor to my specification. The WordPress coaching shifted the quarterly Actuarial Review from an IT-owned publishing cycle to an editorial-owned workflow. IT's involvement dropped from full-cycle publishing to occasional tag verification.

Technical Stack

Drupal 9 (primary editorial platform) · WordPress 5.x (quarterly Actuarial Review) · Composer for dependency management · Git for version control across all environments · Vendor-managed implementation

§ Judgment Call

I chose a two-pronged approach: train the users who were ready to adopt the CMS, and design a bridge for those who were not. The CMS training shipped first because it produced immediate results with no development overhead. For the Drupal editorial accounts, I read the documentation, designed the workflow, and directed the vendor to implement.

The Word-to-CMS plugin was designed as the second phase. A PHP parser for .docx files, leveraging Office Open XML's structured format with predictable style mappings. The plugin would have eliminated the last remaining copy-paste step. Organizational priorities did not align.

In a non-technical organization, I have learned to ship the cultural change first and the automation second. Aided by the official Drupal documentation, I pointed our vendor in the right direction and allowed them to do what they did well — implementation — while I owned the diagnosis, the design, and the verification.
