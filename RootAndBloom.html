Biography

She kept meeting the same patient in three different clinics.

Claire Nowak started in nursing the way most people do — one patient at a time, in a building, on a shift. Pediatrics came first. Then psychiatric mental health. Then a long stretch of watching the same women come back with the same story: the sleep broke, then the mood broke, then the gut broke, and three different specialists each treated their own quarter of it and called the result care.

Nobody was wrong. Everybody was incomplete.

That is the origin of this practice. Not a philosophy she read — a pattern she could not stop seeing. Mind, body, and gut are not three departments. They are one system with three places you can look at it, and if you only look at one, you will keep prescribing for symptoms that have an upstream cause.

Today the practice is solo by design. Every evaluation, every follow-up, every functional consult is Claire. There is no handoff, no covering provider, no fifteen-minute refill queue. Sessions are long because the questions are long.

The record

A career in five movements

Movement I · Foundations

Bedside nursing, then pediatrics

Years of shift work taught the thing no textbook does: families carry the diagnosis long after the discharge paperwork. Pediatric board certification followed, and with it a habit of talking to parents as colleagues rather than obstacles.

Movement II · Psychiatry

Psychiatric mental health, doctorate

A second master's, then a DNP with PMHNP board certification — because treating the mind without the chart, or the chart without the mind, kept producing the same half-answers.

Movement III · The turn

Functional medicine and the gut–brain axis

The patients who got better were the ones whose labs had been read all the way to the bottom. Ferritin. Free T3. B12. Vitamin D. Not exotic — just skipped. Functional training made the skipping stop.

Movement IV · Telehealth

A practice with no waiting room

Utah is big and rural connections are slow, so the practice was built video-first: secure visits, sub-three-second pages, and a clinician who answers her own follow-ups.

Movement V · Now

The serial essays

Numbered installments published almost daily — a thirty-day perimenopause series, a relationships series, and the sentences patients keep asking permission to hear out loud.

In her own words

How Claire writes, and why

Short sentences. No euphemism. If sex hurts, the essay says sex hurts. If you are furious at your husband over the dishwasher, the essay says that is a symptom with a mechanism, not a verdict on your marriage.

The voice is deliberate. People in the middle of a hormonal shift, a sleep-debt crisis, or a caregiving year do not have bandwidth for hedged clinical prose. They need someone to name the thing plainly, tell them it is common, and hand them the next step.

Folio 05Children & Adolescents

Gentle psychiatric care for ages 5‑18 — and the parents who love them.

If you're here, you've probably noticed something in your child that worries you. That worry is a form of love. Let's make a plan together — carefully, safely, and at your child's pace.

Children don't always tell us what's wrong. They show us — through meltdowns, stomachaches, school refusal, withdrawal, or trouble sleeping. The work starts with patience: building trust with your child before asking anything of them, and treating you, the parent, as the expert on your own family.

Medication is never the first word in the conversation with children. It's one tool among several — considered carefully, started low when it's needed, and always paired with family-level support. Many children improve meaningfully with sleep work, routines, school coordination, and gut-aware nutrition changes alone.

How it works

What working together looks like

1. A parent conversation first

We start with you. Tell me what you're seeing at home and school — the full picture, without judgment. Together we decide whether an evaluation makes sense.

2. A child-friendly evaluation

A gentle video visit designed for your child's age and comfort — play-based for younger kids, respectful conversation for teens. You're welcome in the room at every step.

3. A plan you help build

Clear recommendations in plain language — what we'd try first, what we'd watch, and when to check in. You'll never be talked into anything.

Parent guide

Supporting your child at every age

Ages 5‑9

  • Sleep, routines, and play are treatment at this age.
  • We watch growth, appetite, and tummy troubles closely.
  • Medication, when truly needed, starts very low and is monitored closely.
  • Parents are in the room for nearly every visit.

Ages 10‑13

  • Early adolescence brings big shifts in mood, sleep, and identity.
  • We balance parent involvement with growing privacy — with your agreement.
  • School stress, friendships, and screens are part of the conversation.
  • Gut and nutrition habits established now pay off for years.

Ages 14‑18

  • Teens get real autonomy in visits, with safety always first.
  • We screen gently and routinely for safety concerns.
  • College and transition planning is part of care.
  • Teens who understand their own care carry it forward.

Safety comes first — always

Every child and teen is screened gently and routinely for safety concerns, in an age-appropriate way. If your child ever expresses thoughts of self-harm, or you're worried about immediate safety, call or text 988 or call 911 right away — don't wait for an appointment.

Folio 03Our Approach

Mind, body, gut. One system, three places to look.

The logo is not decoration. The rooted tree and the blooming lotus are the two halves of every chart note here — what your body is carrying, and what your mind is carrying — reunited in the care path.

Mind — mood, cognition, stress response

Diagnosis done properly, at length, with the differential actually considered. Depression that is really perimenopause. Anxiety that is really a thyroid. Grief that needs no medication at all and someone to say so.

Body — sleep, hormones, labs, nervous system

Sleep is treated as a vital sign, not a lifestyle complaint. Hormones are read as a curve, not a snapshot. Labs are ordered to the bottom of the panel: thyroid with free T3 and T4, B12, folate, ferritin, vitamin D, A1c, and hormones when the story calls for them.

Gut — the axis nobody refers out

Roughly 90% of the body's serotonin is made in the gut. Bloating, reflux, and bowel changes that arrive in the same season as mood changes are a clinical clue, not a coincidence — and they change the workup.

The difference

Integrative psychiatry versus a standard med check

Same license. Same prescribing authority. A very different fifteen minutes.

The cornerstone

Sleep is often where treatment begins.

Not because sleep is soft or secondary — because everything downstream of it gets harder to read when it is broken. Mood data distorts. Pain thresholds drop. Cognition fogs. Fix the sleep and the rest of the chart starts telling the truth.

  1. Anchor the morning. Outdoor light within 30 minutes of waking — even overcast Utah light is bright enough to set the clock.
  2. Defend the evening. Lights down by 9:40. Screens dimmed or gone. The nervous system needs a runway, not a switch.
  3. Respect the 3 a.m. wake. Out of bed after 20 minutes, dim light, something dull. Bed stays a place for sleep.

What the first 90 days look like

A care path, not a prescription and a goodbye

Week 0 · 60–75 minutes

The long intake

Full history, prior records, every medication and supplement you have tried, sleep architecture, gut history, hormonal timeline. Labs ordered or reviewed. You leave with a written explanation of what is likely happening and why — not just a plan, a rationale.

Weeks 1–4

Sleep and baseline

Circadian work first, medication introduced deliberately and at the lowest useful dose. A two-week symptom-and-food diary if the gut is implicated. Short-interval follow-ups so nothing is guessed at.

Weeks 4–8

Labs back, pattern checked

Results read all the way down the panel. Free T3, ferritin, B12, folate, vitamin D, A1c. Anything deficient is treated — because a normal TSH with a free T3 at the floor is not a normal thyroid, it is a normal reference range.

Weeks 8–12

Consolidate, then space out

What is working stays. What is not gets changed or stopped. Follow-ups move to a sustainable rhythm, and the essays keep arriving for the weeks in between.

Tonight's first step

Sleep is where treatment begins.

You do not have to decide anything about psychiatry tonight. You only have to protect the next eight hours.

  1. 9:40 p.m. — Lights down. Overheads off, lamps only. The clock starts here.
  2. 10:00 p.m. — Screens away or dimmed to warm. Something dull to read instead.
  3. In bed — Four counts in, seven held, eight out. Six rounds. Extended exhale drops heart rate.
  4. 3 a.m. — If you're awake past twenty minutes, get up. Dim light, boring book, back when sleepy.
